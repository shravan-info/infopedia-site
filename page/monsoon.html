A seasonal reversal of winds that delivers most of South Asia's rain between June and September and shapes its agriculture and economy.

Mechanism

A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of prevailing winds caused by the differential heating of land and ocean. In summer the Asian landmass heats far more than the Indian Ocean, the Intertropical Convergence Zone migrates north, and moist air is drawn inland from the sea; in winter the pattern reverses and dry continental air flows outward.1

South Asia's version is amplified by the Tibetan plateau, which acts as an elevated heat source, and by the Mascarene High in the southern Indian Ocean, which sustains the cross-equatorial Somali jet. The result is a two-branch system: the Arabian Sea branch, which strikes the Western Ghats, and the Bay of Bengal branch, which curves up the Gangetic plain and is deflected westward by the Himalaya.

Onset and progress

The India Meteorological Department declares onset over Kerala when a set of criteria on rainfall, wind field and outgoing longwave radiation are met — typically around 1 June, with a normal model error of about a week. The front then advances north, reaching Mumbai in the second week of June, Delhi by the end of the month, and the whole country by mid-July.

Withdrawal begins in the northwest around September and proceeds southeastward, ending over peninsular India in October–November. The intervening break periods, when the monsoon trough shifts to the Himalayan foothills and central India dries out, matter as much for agriculture as the seasonal total.

Variability

Year-to-year variability is driven by large-scale ocean–atmosphere patterns. El Niño years tend to bring below-normal Indian rainfall, though the relationship is not deterministic; the Indian Ocean Dipole can offset or reinforce it, with a positive dipole generally favouring a good monsoon. Madden–Julian Oscillation phases modulate active and break spells on a two-to-six week timescale.

Long-range forecasting has improved but remains probabilistic. IMD issues a seasonal forecast with a model error of roughly ±4 per cent of the long-period average, plus monthly and fortnightly updates, and now runs an ensemble prediction system for the whole season.

Economic and social role

Because roughly half of India's net sown area is unirrigated, monsoon performance feeds directly into agricultural output, rural demand, food prices and hydropower generation. Reservoir inflows, groundwater recharge and the kharif sowing calendar all depend on the distribution of rain rather than only its total.

The same rains cause the disasters: urban flooding, landslides in the Himalayan states, and riverine floods in the Brahmaputra and Ganga basins. Flood management and monsoon forecasting are consequently a single policy problem, and the monsoon's regional shifts under climate change are a live research area, with model projections generally indicating more intense heavy-rain events alongside greater variability in seasonal totals.2
