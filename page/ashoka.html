Mauryan emperor from about 268 to 232 BCE, whose edicts on rock and pillar set out a policy of dhamma after the war in Kalinga.

Accession and empire

Ashoka was the grandson of Chandragupta Maurya, who had founded the dynasty around 321 BCE and, according to later tradition, abdicated in favour of Bindusara. Ashoka's own accession is usually dated to about 268 BCE. Later Buddhist chronicles, notably the Mahavamsa and the Ashokavadana, describe a violent succession struggle, but these accounts were written centuries afterward and their details are disputed.1

At its height the empire stretched from the Hindu Kush and Gandhara to the Godavari, with provincial centres at Taxila, Ujjain, Suvarnagiri and Tosali. Administration relied on a bureaucracy described in Kautilya's Arthashastra and on a network of roads, with the royal capital at Pataliputra on the Ganga.

Kalinga and the turn to dhamma

The thirteenth rock edict records the conquest of Kalinga (roughly modern Odisha) in the eighth year of his reign, with a death toll given in the hundreds of thousands and the emperor's stated remorse at the suffering caused. The authenticity of the numbers is debated — they may be formulaic — but the change in policy that follows is documented across the edicts.

'The Beloved of the Gods, having conquered Kalinga, now feels remorse... This is the chief conquest, namely the conquest by dhamma.' — Thirteenth Rock Edict, c. 261 BCE

Whether Ashoka converted to Buddhism before or after Kalinga is unclear; the edicts themselves speak of dhamma rather than of a sect, and modern scholarship treats the personal-conversion narrative in the Buddhist chronicles with caution.

Dhamma as policy

Ashoka's dhamma is not simply Buddhist doctrine. The edicts emphasise respect for parents and teachers, generosity to brahmins and ascetics of all schools, truthfulness, restraint in violence, and tolerance between religious communities. Officials called dhamma mahamatta were appointed to promote these principles and to report on conditions in the provinces.

The programme also had a concrete welfare dimension: the second rock edict records wells dug, trees planted, and medical treatment provided for people and animals along the roads. Rock edicts prohibit certain sacrificial killings and restrict hunting.

The inscriptions

Ashoka's messages survive on about thirty rock surfaces and a set of polished sandstone pillars, several of which still stand. Most are in Prakrit written in Brahmi; the northwestern texts use Kharoshthi, and the Kandahar bilingual uses Greek and Aramaic. This multilingualism is itself evidence of the empire's reach and administrative sophistication.

The pillars are the technical masterpiece. Each was quarried at Chunar, transported and erected as a single shaft up to about 12 metres, polished to a sheen that has survived twenty-two centuries, and crowned with an animal capital. The Lion Capital of Sarnath — four lions back to back on an abacus with a dharmachakra — was adopted as the emblem of the Republic of India in 1950, and the Ashoka Chakra replaced the charkha at the centre of the national flag.

James Prinsep deciphered Brahmi in 1837, which made the edicts readable and effectively founded the modern study of Ashoka; the identification of 'Devanampiya Piyadasi' as Ashoka followed from the Sri Lankan chronicles.

Legacy

Ashoka's reign is the standard case study in ancient Indian statecraft and in the history of non-violence as policy. Buddhist tradition credits him with convening a council and with sending missions abroad, including one led by his son Mahinda and daughter Sanghamitta to Sri Lanka, an event central to that island's historical narrative.

Modern political uses of Ashoka are numerous and contested — from Nehruvian secularism and the emblem's adoption to Ambedkar's invocation of him for a Buddhist ethical politics. The empire he left fragmented within about fifty years of his death; the edicts, however, outlasted it, and remain the largest corpus of royal inscriptions from the ancient world after those of Persia.
