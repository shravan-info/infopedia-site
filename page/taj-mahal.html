The marble mausoleum at Agra built by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan for Mumtaz Mahal, and the high point of Mughal architecture.

Commission and chronology

Mumtaz Mahal, Shah Jahan's principal consort, died at Burhanpur in June 1631 after the birth of her fourteenth child. Construction of her tomb began at Agra the following year. The mausoleum itself was substantially complete by around 1648, with the surrounding complex — gateway, mosque, guest house and gardens — finished by about 1653.1

The project employed a large workforce over two decades. Court histories and later accounts describe specialist craftsmen brought from across the empire and beyond: stone cutters from Bukhara, calligraphers from Shiraz, inlay workers from southern India. The claim that twenty thousand people and a thousand elephants were used is traditional rather than documented, and modern estimates of the cost vary widely.

The chief architect is conventionally identified as Ustad Ahmad Lahori, though the attribution rests on later sources and the design was clearly a collaborative court enterprise.

Architecture

The complex is arranged on a charbagh grid — a four-part paradise garden divided by water channels — with the mausoleum on a raised plinth at the northern end, on the river terrace overlooking the Yamuna. The composition is strictly symmetrical along one axis, and the corner minarets are canted slightly outward, a detail usually explained as protection for the tomb in the event of collapse.

The central dome rises about 35 metres above the roof and roughly 73 metres above the plinth. Its double-shell construction reduces weight and improves proportions; the four smaller chhatris around it, and the pishtaq portals on each face, repeat the same geometric vocabulary at different scales.

Decorative programme

The surfaces carry three principal decorative techniques: pietra dura inlay of semi-precious stones into marble, low-relief carving of floral motifs, and incised painting. Quranic calligraphy frames the great pishtaqs, with the lettering scaled so that the text appears uniform in size from below — an optical correction that is one of the building's subtler achievements.

Colour shifts with light. The marble takes on a pink cast at dawn, white at midday, and a faint golden or blue tone at dusk; accounts of the effect date from the seventeenth century and remain the most photographed aspect of the monument.

Conservation

Pollution from industry and traffic has yellowed the marble and, at times, promoted algal growth on the northern face. The Archaeological Survey of India has used fullers' earth packs (multani mitti) to draw out deposits, restricted vehicle traffic in the vicinity, and closed or relocated nearby foundries.

Air pollution remains the dominant threat, along with the falling water table of the Yamuna and the pressure of visitor numbers. The monument was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983, and a 10,400 square kilometre trapezoidal 'Taj Trapezium' zone was created in 1998 to control emissions around it.2

Cultural status

The Taj Mahal is India's most visited monument and appears on the ₹1,000 note series introduced in 2023, as well as on postage, in tourism branding and in the standard repertoire of world-heritage photography. It has been a UNESCO site, a Mughal court chronicle subject, and a recurring subject of colonial and modern architectural history — including the persistent myth, without foundation, that it was designed by a European architect.
