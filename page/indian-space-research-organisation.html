India's national space agency, founded in 1969, operator of the PSLV and LVM3 launchers and of the Chandrayaan, Mangalyaan and Aditya missions.

Origins

Indian space activity began with the Indian National Committee for Space Research (INCOSPAR), set up in 1962 under Vikram Sarabhai, who argued that a developing country needed space technology for communications, weather and resource mapping rather than prestige alone. ISRO was constituted on 15 August 1969 and absorbed INCOSPAR's work; the Department of Space was created in 1972.1

Early launches were modest. The first sounding rockets flew from Thumba near Thiruvananthapuram in 1963, with payloads carried to the pad on bicycles and bullock carts — an image that has become part of the agency's institutional memory. Aryabhata, India's first satellite, launched on a Soviet rocket in 1975.

Launch vehicles

ISRO's workhorse is the Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV), first flown in 1993 and since used for Earth observation, Chandrayaan-1 and Mangalyaan, and for commercial rideshare missions that have carried hundreds of foreign satellites. The Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle (GSLV) series provides cryogenic upper stages for heavier payloads, and LVM3 — formerly GSLV Mk III — carries about 4 tonnes to geostationary transfer orbit and 8 tonnes to low Earth orbit.

The Small Satellite Launch Vehicle (SSLV), flown in 2022–23, targets the growing market for light payloads, and a dedicated launch pad at Kulasekarapattinam in Tamil Nadu is intended for small-vehicle polar launches.2

Planetary and solar exploration

Chandrayaan-1 (2008) carried the Moon Impact Probe and the Moon Mineralogy Mapper, whose data established the presence of water molecules and hydroxyl on the lunar surface. The Mars Orbiter Mission, launched in November 2013, entered Mars orbit in September 2014 on a modest budget, making India the first country to succeed on its first attempt and the fourth space agency to reach Mars.3

Chandrayaan-3 landed near the lunar south pole in August 2023, and Aditya-L1, launched the following month, is stationed at the Sun–Earth Lagrange point L1 to observe the solar corona and space weather. A joint NASA–ISRO synthetic aperture radar mission, NISAR, launched in 2025 to map land and ice deformation globally.

Applications and society

ISRO builds and operates the Indian National Satellite (INSAT) system for telecommunications, broadcasting and meteorology, and the Indian Remote Sensing series for agriculture, forestry, water and disaster assessment. NavIC provides regional positioning over India and its neighbourhood.

The agency's social framing has been unusually explicit: satellite-based cyclone warnings, tele-education and telemedicine via EDUSAT and GRAMSAT, and village resource centres were designed as public goods. The 2023 lander cost less than many feature films, a fact widely cited in debates about space spending.

Human spaceflight

Gaganyaan is India's crewed orbital programme, built around a crew module, crew escape system and human-rated LVM3. Uncrewed test flights have been proceeding since 2023, with crewed missions planned for the late 2020s. Rakesh Sharma remains, so far, the only Indian national to have flown in space, aboard Soyuz T-11 in 1984 as part of the Intercosmos programme.

In 2025 the government approved Bharatiya Antariksh Station, a planned Indian space station with a target of the mid-2030s, and an Indian crewed lunar landing ambition beyond that.
