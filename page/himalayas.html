The mountain system formed by the collision of India with Eurasia, home to the highest peaks on Earth and the headwaters of Asia's great rivers.

Formation

The Himalaya are a product of continental collision. The Indian plate, moving north at tens of millimetres a year, met Eurasia roughly 50 million years ago; because neither plate subducts easily, the crust thickened and stacked into thrust sheets. The Main Central Thrust, Main Boundary Thrust and Main Frontal Thrust are the major structures, and the range is still rising as the collision continues — balanced by erosion.1

The uplift also explains the region's seismicity. The 2015 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal (magnitude 7.8) and the 2005 Kashmir earthquake ruptured shallow thrust faults of exactly this kind, and the unbroken segments of the frontal thrusts remain a serious hazard for the densely populated Gangetic plain.

Structure and ranges

Three parallel belts run the length of the system: the Great Himalaya (Himadri), with the highest peaks and permanent snow; the Lesser Himalaya (Himachal), with the Pir Panjal, Dhauladhar and Mahabharat ranges and most of the hill stations; and the Shivalik, the outermost foothills of folded molasse. The Tibetan plateau lies beyond the range's crest, in its rain shadow.

Eight-thousander peaks line the arc: Everest (8,849 m, revised jointly by Nepal and China in 2020), Kangchenjunga (8,586 m), Lhotse, Makalu, Cho Oyu, Dhaulagiri, Manaslu and Annapurna, with K2 and the other Karakoram giants lying in the adjoining system rather than the Himalaya proper.

Climate, glaciers and rivers

The range controls the climate of South Asia. It blocks cold Central Asian air from moving south in winter and forces the summer monsoon to rise and precipitate, so the southern slopes are among the wettest places on Earth while the Tibetan plateau is arid. The monsoon and the westerlies deliver snow in different seasons, and glacier behaviour varies sharply between the eastern and western Himalaya.

Glaciers here feed ten major river systems, including the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra, which together supply water to hundreds of millions of people. Glacial retreat, the growth of unstable moraine-dammed lakes, and the risk of glacial lake outburst floods are documented hazards — the 2021 Chamoli disaster in Uttarakhand, which destroyed two hydropower projects, was one of the most studied recent examples.2

People and ecology

The Himalaya are densely inhabited in the valleys and sparsely above the treeline. Livelihoods combine terraced agriculture, pastoralism, horticulture, tourism and remittances. Cultural and linguistic diversity is extreme: hundreds of languages are spoken across Nepal, Bhutan, the Indian Himalayan states and the Tibetan borderlands, with Hindu, Buddhist, Muslim and animist traditions overlapping in practice.

Ecologically the range spans tropical forest at the base to alpine meadow and permanent ice above 5,000 m, with a treeline that varies with aspect and precipitation. Endemism is high, and protected areas such as Sagarmatha, Khangchendzonga and the Valley of Flowers are World Heritage properties.

Mountaineering

Everest was first climbed on 29 May 1953 by Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay as part of a British expedition led by John Hunt. The 1950s also saw the first ascents of Annapurna (1950) and Kangchenjunga (1955). Commercial expeditions since the 1990s have changed the mountain's character, raising questions about crowding, waste and the ethics of guided climbing at altitude.

Indian mountaineering has its own institutional history through the Himalayan Mountaineering Institute in Darjeeling, founded in 1954, and through the first Indian ascent of Everest in 1965 by a team led by M. S. Kohli.
