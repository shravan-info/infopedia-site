A two-player strategy game of perfect information, descended from Indian chaturanga and now played competitively worldwide and dominated in analysis by engines.

Origins

Chess descends from chaturanga, a four-player game recorded in India by the sixth century CE, whose pieces — infantry, cavalry, elephants and chariotry — map onto the modern pawn, knight, bishop and rook. It travelled to Persia as shatranj, then into the Islamic world and Europe, where the rules were reshaped in the fifteenth century: the queen and bishop gained their long-range powers, and the game acquired the speed and tactical density recognisable today.1

The modern rules were essentially fixed by the nineteenth century, when competitive play, clocks, notation and a body of theory emerged together. The first official world championship match, Steinitz–Zukertort, was played in 1886.

Rules and structure

Each player begins with sixteen pieces. White moves first; the objective is checkmate — attacking the opposing king so that no legal response exists. Special rules include castling, en passant, and pawn promotion. A player may also win on time, or the game may be drawn by stalemate, agreement, threefold repetition, the fifty-move rule or insufficient material.

A game divides into the opening (development and control of the centre), the middlegame (tactics and plans) and the endgame (technique with few pieces). Time controls range from bullet (under three minutes for the whole game) to classical formats of ninety minutes or more per side.

Competitive history

The world championship has passed through distinct eras: the positional school of Steinitz and Lasker, Capablanca's endgame precision, Alekhine's combinative play, the Soviet institutional dominance from Botvinnik to Karpov, and Garry Kasparov's 1985–2000 reign. FIDE, founded in 1924, governs the title and the rating system that replaced earlier informal rankings.

Magnus Carlsen held the title from 2013 to 2023, when he declined to defend it; Ding Liren won the 2023 match, and D. Gukesh took the title in December 2024 at eighteen, the youngest world champion in history. India's rise has been rapid: Viswanathan Anand was world champion from 2007 to 2013, and Indian players now occupy a large share of the top hundred.

The engine era

Deep Blue's match win over Kasparov in 1997 was a landmark, but the deeper shift was quieter: from the mid-2000s, engines such as Rybka, Stockfish and Komodo became stronger than any human, and preparation moved to computer analysis. Opening theory deepened enormously and drawn games at the top level became a recurring complaint.

AlphaZero's 2017 result — learning from the rules alone through self-play and defeating Stockfish in a match — demonstrated a different approach, and its style, with long-term positional sacrifices, influenced human play. Neural-network evaluation is now standard in both commercial and open-source engines.

Chess in India and online

India's chess infrastructure grew through Anand's example, the Chennai-based coaching ecosystem, and a wave of young grandmasters from Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Maharashtra. India won the open section of the Chess Olympiad in 2024, and hosted the event in Chennai in 2022.

Online platforms have changed the game's scale: rapid and blitz formats, streaming commentary, and rating pools of millions of players. FIDE ratings, once purely classical, now extend to faster formats, and online cheating detection has become a live governance problem — one that produced a widely followed controversy in 2022–23.
