A family of Indian practices and philosophies of body and mind, from Patanjali's eight limbs to modern postural yoga.

History

The word yoga derives from the Sanskrit yuj, to join or yoke. Its earliest appearances are in the Rigveda and the Upanishads, where it denotes discipline and inner concentration rather than posture. Seals from the Indus Valley Civilisation showing a cross-legged figure are often described as proto-yogic, but the identification is speculative and contested.1

The classical formulation is Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, a terse text of about 196 aphorisms dated variously between the second century BCE and the fourth century CE. It defines yoga as the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind and sets out an eight-limbed path. The Bhagavad Gita presents yoga as a family of disciplines — action, devotion, knowledge — and the Yoga Vasistha and later tantric literature expand the philosophical frame.

Posture-centred practice is comparatively late. The Hatha Yoga Pradipika of Svatmarama (fifteenth century) and the Gheranda Samhita describe many asanas, cleansing techniques and breath control, with aims that are soteriological rather than athletic.

The eight limbs

Patanjali's ashtanga — eight limbs, not to be confused with the modern Ashtanga Vinyasa style — runs from ethics to absorption: the restraints (yama) and observances (niyama); posture (asana); breath regulation (pranayama); withdrawal of the senses (pratyahara); concentration (dharana); meditation (dhyana); and samadhi. Only two of the eight concern the body directly, which is why historians of the tradition resist reducing yoga to exercise.

Modern postural yoga

The physical yoga practised globally today took shape in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, through a mixture of Indian revivalism, nationalism, Western physical culture and gymnastics. Swami Vivekananda's 1893 address in Chicago introduced Vedanta and Raja Yoga to a Western audience; later teachers such as Swami Kuvalayananda and Sri Yogendra pursued scientific study of the practices.

T. Krishnamacharya, teaching in Mysore from the 1930s, trained a generation — K. Pattabhi Jois (Ashtanga Vinyasa), B. K. S. Iyengar (alignment-based practice), T. K. V. Desikachar (Viniyoga) and Indra Devi, who carried the practice to the West. Their styles, plus Sivananda yoga and Bikram's heat-based sequence, account for most contemporary classes.

Scholarship — notably Mark Singleton's Yoga Body (2010) — has documented how much of modern postural practice derives from nineteenth-century Indian physical culture rather than unbroken transmission from antiquity, a finding that is accepted by most academic historians and disputed by some traditionalist lineages.

Health evidence and policy

Clinical research on yoga has grown substantially, with systematic reviews reporting modest benefits for chronic low back pain, stress and anxiety measures, sleep quality and some cardiovascular risk factors. Trial quality is uneven — small samples, short follow-up and difficulty blinding — so claims of large effects should be read cautiously.

In India, yoga is embedded in public health and education policy through the Ministry of Ayush, the Morarji Desai National Institute of Yoga, and the International Day of Yoga on 21 June, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in December 2014 and observed from 2015. The day has become the tradition's largest global public event.
