A framework for updating beliefs with evidence, in which a prior distribution and a likelihood combine into a posterior.

The theorem and its use

Bayes' theorem relates the probability of a hypothesis given data to the probability of the data given the hypothesis: p(\theta \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(x)} The denominator is a normalising constant, so in practice inference works with the proportionality p(θ|x) ∝ p(x|θ) p(θ).1

Applied iteratively, the posterior after one experiment becomes the prior for the next, which gives the framework its natural treatment of sequential learning. The output is a full distribution rather than a point estimate, so uncertainty is explicit: a 95 per cent credible interval is read directly as the region containing the parameter with 95 per cent posterior probability.

History

Thomas Bayes's essay, published posthumously by Richard Price in 1763, solved a special case involving a uniform prior. Pierre-Simon Laplace developed the general form and applied it to astronomy, demography and jurisprudence in the late eighteenth century; the modern name is a nineteenth-century attribution to a narrower result.

The approach fell out of favour in early twentieth-century statistics, partly because the choice of prior seemed subjective and partly because the integrals were intractable. It returned with computing: Metropolis and colleagues' 1953 algorithm, Hastings's 1970 generalisation, the Geman brothers' Gibbs sampler in 1984, and Gelfand and Smith's 1990 synthesis made posterior simulation routine.

Priors and their critics

Priors encode what is known before the data arrive. Conjugate priors — a beta prior with a binomial likelihood, for instance — yield posteriors in closed form. Weakly informative priors stabilise estimates without asserting much. Empirical Bayes estimates the prior from the data itself.

The frequentist objection is that conclusions should not depend on information not contained in the data, and that a determined analyst can shift a result by choosing a prior. Bayesian practice answers that priors should be declared in advance, checked by sensitivity analysis, and that every statistical method embeds assumptions somewhere — the advantage is that these are stated explicitly rather than hidden in a test procedure.2

Applications

Bayesian methods underpin clinical trial designs with adaptive stopping rules, spam filtering, recommender systems, phylogenetics, epidemiology, machine learning (Bayesian neural networks, Gaussian processes, variational autoencoders) and the hierarchical models used across the social sciences. Probabilistic programming languages such as Stan and PyMC made these models accessible to non-specialists.
