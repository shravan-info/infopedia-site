A region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, including light, can escape once it crosses the event horizon.

Definition and the event horizon

A black hole is not a solid object but a region of spacetime whose curvature is extreme enough that every future-directed path inside a boundary — the event horizon — leads inward. The horizon is a mathematical surface, not a physical membrane; an observer falling through it would notice nothing locally special, though they could no longer send signals out.1

For a non-rotating, uncharged hole the horizon sits at the Schwarzschild radius, r_s = 2GM/c^2 which works out to roughly 3 kilometres per solar mass. A hole with the mass of the Sun would therefore be about 6 km across; one with the mass of the Earth, about 9 mm.

Real holes rotate. The Kerr solution, published by Roy Kerr in 1963, describes spinning holes with an ergosphere outside the horizon, a region where no observer can remain stationary with respect to distant stars.

Formation

Stellar-mass black holes form when a massive star (roughly above 20 solar masses) exhausts its fuel and its core collapses. If the remnant exceeds the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit of about 2–2.5 solar masses, no known force can halt the collapse. The accompanying supernova often, but not always, expels the outer envelope.

Supermassive black holes, with masses from millions to tens of billions of solar masses, sit at the centres of most large galaxies. Their origin is unresolved: candidates include direct collapse of massive gas clouds, runaway mergers of stellar-mass holes, and dense nuclear star clusters. The James Webb Space Telescope's discovery of very massive, very early quasars has sharpened the question, since some appear too large for their epoch under simple growth models.2

How they are observed

Black holes are detected through their effects on surrounding matter. Accretion disks reach temperatures of millions of kelvin and radiate in X-rays; Sagittarius A* at the Milky Way's centre was identified from the orbits of stars whipping around an invisible mass, work that earned Andrea Ghez and Reinhard Genzel a share of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.3

Gravitational waves offer a direct channel. The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory detected merging black holes on 14 September 2015 (GW150914), confirming a prediction of general relativity and opening a new astronomy; the 2017 Nobel Prize followed.4

Imaging arrived in 2019, when the Event Horizon Telescope — a very-long-baseline array spanning the globe — published the shadow of M87*, a 6.5-billion-solar-mass hole in the Virgo cluster. In 2022 the same collaboration imaged Sagittarius A*, four million solar masses and far harder to reconstruct because it changes minute by minute.5

Thermodynamics and information

Black holes behave thermodynamically. Jacob Bekenstein proposed that entropy scales with horizon area rather than volume, and Stephen Hawking showed in 1974 that quantum field theory in curved spacetime implies thermal emission at a temperature inversely proportional to mass — Hawking radiation.6

For astrophysical holes this radiation is absurdly faint, far below the cosmic microwave background, so it has never been observed. Its theoretical importance is large: it suggests holes evaporate, and it creates the information paradox, since the radiation appears featureless while quantum mechanics requires unitary evolution. Resolutions proposed over five decades include complementarity, firewalls and holographic dualities, none yet settled by experiment.
