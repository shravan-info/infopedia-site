Computation built on quantum states — superposition, entanglement and interference — with the promise of exponential speed-ups for specific problems.

Principles

A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit holds a state |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle with complex amplitudes whose squared magnitudes sum to one. Measurement returns a single bit with probability given by those amplitudes and destroys the superposition, so the power of a quantum computer is not 'trying every answer at once' but engineering interference so that wrong paths cancel before measurement.

Entanglement correlates qubits so that their joint state cannot be factored into independent parts. It is the resource behind teleportation, dense coding and most speed-ups, and it is fragile: any coupling to the environment leaks information and degrades the state.1

Algorithms with proven advantage

Shor's algorithm factors integers in polynomial time, which would break RSA and Diffie–Hellman if run at sufficient scale. Grover's algorithm gives a quadratic speed-up for unstructured search. Quantum simulation of molecules and materials is the application most experts consider both natural and likely to arrive first, since a quantum system is the obvious simulator of another quantum system.2

The list of proven exponential advantages is short. Hype often outruns it: for many optimisation problems the quantum advantage is either unproven or vanishes against better classical heuristics, and the field has a documented history of overclaiming.

Hardware

Superconducting transmon circuits are the most developed platform: lithographic qubits operating at millikelvin temperatures, with gate times in nanoseconds and coherence times in the tens to hundreds of microseconds. Trapped ions offer longer coherence and all-to-all connectivity at the cost of slower gates. Photonic systems promise room-temperature operation and networking, while neutral atoms offer large, reconfigurable arrays.

The DiVincenzo criteria — scalable qubits, initialisation, long coherence, universal gates, and measurement — remain the checklist. No platform satisfies all of them at the scale required for useful fault tolerance.

Error correction

Quantum error correction encodes one logical qubit across many physical ones so that errors can be detected and corrected without measuring the encoded state. The surface code is the leading candidate. The critical threshold — the physical error rate below which adding qubits reduces logical error — is around 1 per cent for the surface code.

Google's Willow processor, announced in December 2024, demonstrated below-threshold operation: doubling the code distance reduced logical error, the first clear experimental evidence of the effect. The gap to useful computation remains large, since a practical logical qubit may need thousands of physical qubits and algorithms need thousands of logical ones.3

Outlook

John Preskill's 'NISQ' framing — noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices without full error correction — describes the current decade. Near-term claims centre on chemistry, materials and specialised sampling. Timelines for cryptographically relevant machines are contested, with estimates ranging from a decade to considerably longer.

Post-quantum cryptography is the field's most concrete consequence so far: NIST standardised the first post-quantum algorithms in 2024 precisely because encrypted data recorded today could be decrypted later by a future machine.
