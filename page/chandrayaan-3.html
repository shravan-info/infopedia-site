India's 2023 lunar mission, the first to land softly near the Moon's south pole, carrying the Vikram lander and Pragyan rover.

Mission profile

Chandrayaan-3 was ISRO's follow-up to Chandrayaan-2, whose lander was lost during descent in September 2019. The third mission dropped the orbiter and concentrated on a robust landing, with a propulsion module that carried the lander to a 100 km circular orbit and then performed its own extended observations.1

The spacecraft launched on 14 July 2023 aboard LVM3-M4 and spent about forty days raising its orbit around Earth before a trans-lunar injection on 1 August. It entered lunar orbit on 5 August and progressively lowered its perilune to roughly 25 km, where the lander separated on 17 August.

The lander carried four hazard-avoidance and velocity-measurement sensors, a laser Doppler velocimeter and an improved guidance algorithm with multiple fallback paths — design changes attributed directly to the Chandrayaan-2 failure.

The landing

Vikram touched down at 18:04 IST on 23 August 2023, in the high-latitude southern region rather than at the pole itself. The site was named Shiv Shakti Point, and 23 August is observed as National Space Day in India. India became the fourth country to soft-land on the Moon after the Soviet Union, the United States and China, and the first to land in the southern high latitudes.2

'The lander has touched down on the Moon.' — ISRO mission control, 23 August 2023

The Pragyan rover, about 26 kg and solar-powered, drove down a ramp and covered a few dozen metres over roughly two weeks of lunar daylight before entering the cold night. It was not designed to survive the two-week night, and contact was not re-established.

Instruments and findings

The lander's ChaSTE probe measured a surface temperature of about 50 °C and roughly −10 °C at 8 cm depth, an unexpectedly steep near-surface gradient. The ILSA instrument recorded seismic activity; RAMBHA-LP measured plasma density near the surface; and the Langmuir probe and radio anatomy experiment contributed to studies of the lunar ionosphere.

Pragyan's Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy instrument identified sulphur in the regolith alongside aluminium, calcium, chromium, iron, manganese, silicon, titanium and oxygen. The Alpha Particle X-ray Spectrometer corroborated the elemental composition. The discovery of sulphur at high latitude was significant because it is volatile and its distribution bears on lunar geology and possible resource use.3

The propulsion module's SHAPE payload observed Earth from lunar orbit, and in late 2023 the module was returned from lunar orbit to a high Earth orbit — an unplanned manoeuvre that demonstrated propellant-efficient trajectory design.

Significance

The mission's cost, reported at roughly ₹615 crore, made it a reference point in global discussions of low-cost spaceflight. Scientifically, the southern high latitudes are the most promising region for water ice, which would matter for life support and propellant production; Chandrayaan-3's data add ground truth for the orbital observations made since Chandrayaan-1.

The mission also fed directly into India's subsequent programme: it validated landing technology intended for a sample-return mission and for the crewed lunar ambitions announced alongside the Bharatiya Antariksh Station plans.
