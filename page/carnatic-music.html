The classical music tradition of southern India, organised around raga, tala and a composed repertoire centred on the kriti.

History

Carnatic music descends from the same Sanskrit theoretical tradition as Hindustani music: the Natyashastra, the raga–tala framework of Matanga's Brihaddeshi, and the medieval Sangita Ratnakara. The southern tradition diverged from the fourteenth century onward, developing its own repertoire and pedagogy.1

Purandara Dasa (1484–1564) is conventionally called the Sangita Pitamaha, the father of the system, for codifying elementary exercises (sarali varisai, alankaras) and composing thousands of devotional songs. The seventeenth-century theorist Venkatamakhin set out the 72 melakarta scheme of parent scales that remains the classificatory backbone.

The late eighteenth century produced the 'trinity' — Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar and Syama Sastri — whose compositions in Telugu and Sanskrit form the core of the modern concert repertoire. Swati Tirunal in Travancore added another substantial body of work in the nineteenth century.

Raga

A raga is a melodic entity defined by a scale, characteristic phrases, ornamentation and rules of movement. It is not simply a mode: two ragas may share a scale and differ entirely in their ascent, descent and emphasis. The 72 melakarta scheme orders parent scales by the arrangement of the twelve semitones, and janya ragas derive from them by omitting or varying degrees.

Ornament is central. Gamaka — oscillations, slides and deflections of pitch — carries much of a raga's identity, and notation can only approximate it. This is why the tradition is transmitted orally, through a guru–shishya lineage, rather than from scores.

Tala and rhythm

Tala is the metric cycle. Seven basic talas (dhruva, matya, rupaka, jhampa, triputa, ata, eka) combine with five variants of the internal unit to give 35, and each may be performed at different speeds. The cycle is marked by hand gestures — beats and waves — that both the percussionist and the singer follow.

Improvisation takes several forms: alapana, an unmetered exposition of a raga; neraval, improvised variation on a line of a composition within the tala; kalpanaswara, solfège passages in the raga; and tanam, a rhythmic non-metrical style associated with the veena. A concert typically ends with a ragam–tanam–pallavi, the most demanding improvised form.

Composition forms

The kriti is a three-part devotional composition — pallavi (refrain), anupallavi (second section) and charanam (verses) — usually with a melodic signature (mudra) naming the composer. Varnam, a study piece that lays out a raga's shape; swarajati; javali and padam, the latter especially in the padam tradition of Kshetrayya; and the devotional group compositions of Tyagaraja's Pancharatna kritis are also standard.

The repertoire is overwhelmingly devotional, addressed to a named deity in the composer's ishta devata tradition, but the concert is a concert: a secular, ticketed, professional performance format developed in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Performance culture

The modern concert format — roughly two to three hours, a main item with elaborate improvisation, and lighter pieces at the close — took shape in the early twentieth century alongside the Madras Music Season, held each December and anchored since 1928 by the Madras Music Academy. The Academy's annual conference also functioned as a standardising body for raga classification and performance practice.

Veena, violin (adopted from Western music and now central), flute and nagaswaram carry melody; mridangam, ghatam, kanjira and morsing carry rhythm; the tanpura provides the drone. Amplification, recording and diaspora teaching have reshaped the tradition in the last fifty years, as has the growth of an international concert circuit.
