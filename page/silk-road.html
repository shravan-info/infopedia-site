The network of Eurasian land and sea routes that carried goods, religions and technologies between China, India, Persia, Arabia and Europe.

Naming and geography

The Silk Road was never a single road. It is a shorthand for a shifting web of caravan tracks, river crossings and oasis towns linking the Chinese interior to Central Asia, Persia, the Levant and the Mediterranean, with a parallel maritime network through the Indian Ocean, the Malacca Strait and the Red Sea.1

The name was coined in 1877 by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen, long after the routes had declined, and it overstates silk's share of trade — much of the traffic was in horses, livestock, grain, metals, glass, aromatics and, above all, ordinary local exchange between adjacent regions.

Han expansion

The Han dynasty's missions under Zhang Qian (138–126 BCE) to the Yuezhi, undertaken to find allies against the Xiongnu, returned with intelligence about Central Asian states and their markets. Chinese expansion into the Hexi Corridor followed, with the garrison system that anchored Dunhuang and the Jade Gate.

Parthian and later Sogdian merchants dominated the middle sections. Sogdian traders from Samarkand were the era's great intermediaries, with colonies as far east as Chang'an; their letters, preserved by chance in a desert watchtower, are among the most vivid documents of everyday commerce on the routes.

Trade and technology

Caravans moved in stages rather than end to end: goods changed hands many times, so the merchant who bought silk in Chang'an rarely saw Rome. Oasis cities taxed and provisioned the traffic, and their fortunes tracked the security of the passes.

Technology travelled more consequentially than luxury goods. Papermaking reached the Islamic world in the eighth century — traditionally associated with craftsmen taken at the Battle of Talas in 751 — and from there Europe. Sericulture moved west slowly, gunpowder and printing followed, and glassmaking, cotton and metallurgy moved in the other direction.

Religions and ideas

Buddhism spread from India into Central Asia and China along these routes from the first century CE, carried by monks and merchants; the cave temples at Dunhuang, Bamiyan and Kizil are its monuments. Manichaeism, Nestorian Christianity and Islam later travelled the same corridors, and the transmission of Indian mathematics, astronomy and medicine into the Abbasid world ran through the same channels.

The Dunhuang library cave, sealed around the eleventh century and reopened in 1900, yielded tens of thousands of manuscripts in Chinese, Tibetan, Sogdian, Uighur and Sanskrit — the single richest archive of the network's cultural life.

Decline and revival

The Mongol unification of the thirteenth century briefly made the overland routes safer, and travellers such as Rabban Bar Sauma, William of Rubruck and Marco Polo described them. Fragmentation after the Mongol collapse, the rise of European maritime power, and the Portuguese entry into the Indian Ocean after 1498 shifted trade decisively to the sea.

The phrase returned to politics in the twentieth century and again in 2013, when China announced the Belt and Road Initiative, which explicitly invokes the Silk Road in naming its overland and maritime corridors. Historians generally caution that the modern analogy is rhetorical: the medieval network was decentralised, private and unplanned in a way that centralised infrastructure programmes are not.2
