A neural network trained on very large text corpora to predict and generate language, the architecture behind modern conversational AI.

Architecture

Nearly all current large language models are decoder-only Transformers. Text is first split into tokens by a subword tokeniser, embedded as vectors, and passed through a stack of blocks each containing multi-head self-attention and a feed-forward network with residual connections and normalisation. Self-attention lets every position weigh every earlier position, which is what allows long-range dependencies to be modelled in parallel rather than sequentially.1

The attention operation is \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V where Q, K and V are learned projections of the input. Rotary positional embeddings, grouped-query attention, and mixture-of-experts feed-forward layers are common efficiency modifications: mixture-of-experts activates only a subset of parameters per token, so a model can have a very large total parameter count and a much smaller active one.

Training pipeline

Pretraining minimises cross-entropy on the next token over trillions of tokens drawn from web crawls, books, code and curated data. The resulting base model is a powerful autocomplete but a poor assistant: it will continue a question rather than answer it.

Instruction tuning then fine-tunes on demonstrations of tasks, and preference optimisation — reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), or the simpler direct preference optimisation (DPO) — aligns outputs with annotator preferences. Constitutional AI and rule-based reward models are variants that reduce reliance on human labelling.2

The 'emergent abilities' story — that certain skills appear abruptly past a scale threshold — has been challenged. Schaeffer et al. (2023) argued that apparent emergence is largely an artefact of discontinuous metrics such as exact-match accuracy.3

Capabilities and limits

Modern models write and refactor code, summarise and translate, answer questions over long documents, and drive tool calls. Context windows have grown from a few thousand tokens to hundreds of thousands and, in some systems, millions, though effective use of very long contexts degrades unevenly with position.

The central weakness is hallucination: because the objective rewards plausible continuations, a model can produce fluent, specific, wrong statements, including fabricated citations and case law. Retrieval augmentation, tool use, and trained abstention reduce but do not eliminate this. Other limits include weak multi-step arithmetic without tooling, sensitivity to prompt phrasing, and the difficulty of verifying that a model is not reproducing memorised private data.

Evaluation and openness

Benchmarks such as MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval and their successors are widely reported and increasingly contaminated by appearing in training data. Human preference arenas provide a complementary signal. Neither measures reliability in deployment, which is why domain-specific evaluations and red-teaming have become standard practice.

The field divides over openness. Some labs release weights and technical reports; others expose only APIs. Arguments for open weights emphasise auditability, cost and decentralised innovation; arguments against emphasise misuse risk, the impossibility of un-releasing a model, and the concentration of capability in whoever can afford the compute.
