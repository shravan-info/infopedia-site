The light-driven conversion of carbon dioxide and water into sugars, the reaction that supplies nearly all energy to life on Earth.

Overview

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae and cyanobacteria capture light energy and store it as chemical bonds in carbohydrates. In oxygenic photosynthesis the inputs are carbon dioxide, water and light; the outputs are sugar and molecular oxygen. The overall reaction is usually summarised as 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light → C6H12O6 + 6 O2, although the true pathway runs through dozens of enzyme-catalysed steps rather than a single equation.1

The process matters beyond plant biology. Oxygenic photosynthesis produced the oxygen atmosphere roughly 2.4 billion years ago during the Great Oxidation Event, and it still fixes on the order of 100–130 gigatonnes of carbon each year, forming the base of nearly every food web.2

Light-dependent reactions

The light reactions take place in and on the thylakoid membranes stacked inside the chloroplast. Photosystem II absorbs light at a reaction centre known as P680, splits water through the oxygen-evolving complex, and releases electrons, protons and oxygen. The electrons travel down an electron transport chain — plastoquinone, the cytochrome b6f complex, plastocyanin — pumping protons into the thylakoid lumen.3

Photosystem I then re-energises the electrons at P700 and passes them to ferredoxin and finally to NADP+ reductase, producing NADPH. The proton gradient built across the membrane drives ATP synthase, generating ATP. Both products, ATP and NADPH, are consumed in the next stage.

The oxygen released by plants comes from water, not from carbon dioxide. Samuel Ruben and Martin Kamen established this in 1941 using the heavy oxygen isotope O-18 as a tracer.4

The Calvin–Benson cycle

The Calvin–Benson cycle runs in the stroma and fixes carbon without directly using light. The enzyme RuBisCO attaches CO2 to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate, producing two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate. ATP and NADPH from the light reactions reduce these to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, most of which regenerates the starting material while a fraction is exported to build sucrose and starch.5

RuBisCO is thought to be the most abundant protein on Earth, and also one of the slowest enzymes. Its dual affinity for oxygen — photorespiration — wastes fixed carbon, which is why several plant lineages evolved workarounds.

Discovery and research

Jan Ingenhousz showed in 1779 that plants release oxygen only in light. Joseph Priestley had earlier demonstrated that a plant could restore air spoiled by a burning candle. In 1845 Julius von Sachs and others established that starch accumulates in illuminated leaves, and the term photosynthesis entered use in the 1890s.

Twentieth-century work mapped the machinery: Melvin Calvin traced the carbon pathway with radioactive carbon-14 and received the 1961 Nobel Prize in Chemistry; Robert Emerson's experiments on red drop and enhancement led to the two-photosystem model; and Daniel Arnon reconstituted photophosphorylation in isolated chloroplasts.6

Global significance

Photosynthesis links the carbon cycle, the climate system and agriculture. Land plants take up a substantial share of human CO2 emissions each year, and ocean phytoplankton account for roughly half of global primary production. Because RuBisCO is imperfect, researchers have pursued engineering routes — from improved C4 crops to artificial photosynthesis — to raise yields and store carbon.

Understanding the process also underpins attempts at solar fuels: splitting water with sunlight and catalysts to make hydrogen or reduced carbon compounds, a design that natural photosynthesis already performs at ambient temperature.
